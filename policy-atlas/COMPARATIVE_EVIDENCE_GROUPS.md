# Comparative evidence groups

**Evidence freeze: 28 August 2026 · working synthesis · not peer reviewed**

## The correction

The Atlas does **not** identify six effective policies.

The six records previously foregrounded were selected because they carried the strongest prior component-effect claims. Re-audit narrowed or reclassified all six. They are an audit sample, not a shortlist, and `6/118` must not be interpreted as the number of interventions that work.

The 118 implementations are instead organised into six **recommendation postures**. These compare the present strength and directness of support, the CDE node targeted, operational maturity, reversibility, rights costs, and the evidence needed to upgrade a claim. They are not effect sizes or a scalar ranking.

## What is comparatively best supported

The strongest current empirical support is for **narrow component effects**, not for end-to-end protection from adaptive AI manipulation. The leading set is therefore small and specific: restrictions on manipulative choice architecture, friction on rapid redistribution, technique-based prebunking, grounding of election-administration answers in authoritative sources, and limits on unnecessary personal-data retention or use.

These controls should be recommended because they have better support **relative to the rest of the portfolio**, not because they have demonstrated preservation of democratic autonomy, prevention of agency transfer, or changed election outcomes.

The next most defensible priority is not another content label. It is **control and observability infrastructure**: scoped permissions, independent access, action confirmation, traces, incident response, evidence custody, procurement exit, and continuity. Their manipulation-specific effect evidence is thinner, but their causal fit is strong and they preserve the ability to detect, interrupt, contest, and reverse concentrated control.

## Agency-transfer interpretation

A control should be evaluated at the node it directly changes:

```text
AI capability → controller → influence vector → target → change in belief,
attention, trust, behaviour or dependency → agency transfer → concentration
of power → democratic harm → mitigation
```

A forwarding limit may reduce rapid redistribution. A dark-pattern rule may reduce distorted consent. A provenance rule may improve attribution. A least-privilege architecture may prevent autonomous escalation. None of these endpoints, alone, establishes that voters retained reflective agency or that an election was protected.

## Decision rule

1. **Act on the evidenced mechanism.** Do not promote a component result into an election-effect claim.
2. **Prefer narrow, reversible, monitorable controls under uncertainty.**
3. **Combine layers.** Group A without Group B is easy to route around; Group B without legal baselines may remain voluntary; Group D without the other groups is mostly observability theatre.
4. **Pilot frontier-specific controls before mandating them broadly.**
5. **Hold general thresholds until the construct, calibration, comparator, and action rule are validated.**

## Method boundary

This grouping is a comparative synthesis of the current Atlas and its claim re-audit. It is **not** a completed 118-intervention meta-analysis with independent double screening and full-text adjudication for every source. Candidate sources do not upgrade an intervention until their design, endpoint, comparator, population, and causal match have been checked.
